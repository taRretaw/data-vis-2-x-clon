South-East Australian National Parks

Spatial distribution, trail density, and amenity metrics across NSW, VIC, and TAS.

South-east Australia is a region of varied terrain and striking scenery. The Great Dividing Range runs down the east coast of New South Wales (NSW) and on into Victoria, ending at the Grampians in the west, while Tasmania holds some of the continent’s last untamed wilderness.

Many of Australia’s most treasured national parks lie here. National parks come in all sizes. NSW has by far the most, and many cover less than 100 km². Victoria has fewer, and Tasmania fewest of all, though its parks tend to be larger and more remote.

Exploring National Parks by Foot or Car?

National parks give us scenery to explore, whether on foot or by four-wheel drive. Of the five parks in this story, the Blue Mountains and the Grampians have the most track for their size, measured as metres of track per square kilometre. Franklin-Gordon Wild Rivers, by contrast, is largely untracked.

The Grampians Peaks Trail runs from Mount Zero in the north to Dunkeld in the south. It takes about 13 days to walk and is among the longest walking routes inside a single national park in south-east Australia.

The profile shows a trail of repeated climbs and descents. The biggest climb comes around the 60 km mark, where it crosses Durd Durd and Mt William, the highest points at 1,167 m.

The Most Mountainous Parks

Height changes everything for a walker: colder weather, thinner soils and views that reward the effort. Mount Kosciuszko, in the Australian Alps, is the tallest peak on the continent, and it draws visitors from all over the world. Part of the appeal is the pull of extremes, the urge to stand on the highest point.

Each lollipop is the highest peak in a park. Kosciuszko and the Alpine park clearly lead. The Grampians, Blue Mountains and Wild Rivers are lower than the great ranges of Nepal, Europe or the Andes, but they offer something different: glacial lakes, rooftop plateaus and unique flora.

The Peaks of Alpine and Kosciuszko National Parks

The high country straddles the NSW–Victoria border, where the Murray River begins. Most of Australia's tallest peaks cluster around Kosciuszko.

The triangles are every named peak in OpenStreetMap, sized by height. Kosciuszko itself is a broad, gentle summit rather than a sharp spike. No Victorian peak reaches 2,000 m, and Mount Bogong is the state's highest. The labelled peaks are the ones best known for hiking routes.

The two parks sit on opposite sides of the border: Kosciuszko in NSW and Alpine in Victoria. Together they form the core of the Australian Alps, the only part of the continent that gets reliable winter snow, which is why the ski resorts of Thredbo, Perisher, Falls Creek and Mount Hotham sit here.

Many of the labelled peaks come with a classic walk. Kosciuszko can be reached from Thredbo or Charlotte Pass in a day, while Mount Bogong and Mount Feathertop in Victoria are known for long, steep ridge climbs. Mount Jagungal, to the north, is more remote and sees far fewer walkers.

Franklin-Gordon’s rivers are best explored by kayak. The low, wet terrain makes it almost impossible to maintain walking tracks, and most rivers flow west towards Macquarie Harbour.

The Blue Mountains offers a different kind of water. Waterfalls cluster around Wentworth Falls and Katoomba in the centre of the park, and make destinations for weekend and day trips from Sydney.
